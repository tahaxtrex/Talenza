PART 1 — THE PROBLEM SPEECH

Use this for the 0:00–0:20 section of your video

Short Version (20 seconds — memorise this exactly)

"Intuition drives 85 to 97 percent of hiring decisions at the manager level.
48 percent of HR professionals have admitted that personal bias directly affected
their candidate choice. And when organisations do use rating systems,
research shows the dominant pattern is centrality bias —
everyone scores a 7 or an 8, so the data becomes useless for differentiation.
At BMW's scale, where a wrong leadership hire can destroy a strong system,
these are not acceptable odds.
We built ContextHire to replace the gut feel with a reason."

Full Version (for Q&A — use this if judges ask about the problem)

"Let us ground this in research, not assumption.

A LinkedIn analysis of over 1,000 hiring managers found that between
85 and 97 percent of hiring decisions are driven primarily by intuition
rather than structured data — and this figure is even higher for
senior executive appointments, where the stakes are largest.

When organisations attempt to introduce structure through rating systems,
they encounter a well-documented cognitive phenomenon called centrality bias —
also known as central tendency bias. HR Dive defines it as the tendency
to rate most candidates in the middle of a scale, making it impossible
to distinguish top performers from average ones.
BMW's own slides put it bluntly: everyone is a 7 or an 8.
That is not a data problem. That is a decision architecture problem.

A peer-reviewed quantitative study published in the German Journal of
Human Resource Management, surveying 234 HR professionals in Switzerland,
found something even more troubling: HR employees systematically underestimate
their own susceptibility to bias while overestimating their colleagues'.
This is called the bias blind spot — and it means the people making
the most consequential hiring decisions are the least likely to believe
they need help making them better.

The business cost is not abstract.
McKinsey's research on executive succession has consistently shown
that the wrong senior leader costs organisations between 1.5 and 2 times
that leader's annual salary in replacement costs alone — before accounting
for the operational damage, team turnover, and cultural regression
that follow a misaligned appointment.

ContextHire does not ask for ratings.
It asks for evidence.
And it surfaces decisions that are context-aware — because the right leader
for a digital transformation is not the same person you need in a production crisis."

2A — Why Claude Sonnet 4.6 for the Core Agents

Short Version (15 seconds per agent — pick what fits)

"For the agents that require structured reasoning and business-context judgment,
we chose Claude Sonnet 4.6. According to Anthropic's official benchmarks,
published February 17, 2026, Sonnet 4.6 leads every model in the world
on GDPval-AA — the benchmark that specifically measures expert-level
office and knowledge work — with an Elo score of 1,633.
That is 27 points above Opus 4.6, and 316 points above Gemini 3.1 Pro.
This is exactly the category our agents operate in."

Full Version (for Q&A)

"Let me be precise about why we chose Sonnet 4.6 and not a more recent or
more expensive model.

We evaluated four criteria: task alignment, benchmark performance on our
specific workload type, production stability, and cost predictability
during a live demo.

On task alignment: our agents perform structured knowledge work.
They take a business context, reason about trade-offs, produce scored outputs
with evidence, and generate comparative recommendations. This is not
abstract reasoning or scientific depth — it is expert-level office cognition.

The benchmark that measures exactly this is GDPval-AA, the gold standard
for real-world office productivity. On GDPval-AA, as of February 17, 2026,
Claude Sonnet 4.6 scores 1,633 Elo — the highest of any model tested,
including its own flagship Opus 4.6 at 1,606.
Gemini 3.1 Pro scores 1,317 — 316 Elo points behind.

On SWE-bench Verified, which measures real-world software engineering
using actual GitHub issues, Sonnet 4.6 scores 79.6% — within 1.2
percentage points of Opus 4.6 at 80.8%, at one-fifth the cost.

On production stability: Sonnet 4.6 is generally available as of
February 17, 2026. Gemini 3.1 Pro — while impressive on abstract reasoning
benchmarks — is still in preview as of today, March 27, 2026.
Published independent testing found response times exceeding 100 seconds
under load at launch. For a live demo where the scenario toggle is
our primary demonstration, that latency is an unacceptable risk.

On cost: Sonnet 4.6 is priced at $3 per million input tokens —
identical to Sonnet 4.5 despite significant benchmark improvements.
This means we run six parallel profile scoring calls at Haiku 4.5
pricing ($0.25 per million tokens) for speed, and use Sonnet 4.6
for the reasoning-heavy agents where output quality is visible
to the judges.

One last point on model choice integrity.
We also evaluated GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3.1 Pro.
GPT-5.2 scores 38.2% on OSWorld-Verified for agentic computer use,
compared to Sonnet 4.6's 72.5%. It is not the right tool for
sustained agentic workflows.
Gemini 3.1 Pro leads on abstract reasoning at 77.1% on ARC-AGI-2,
but that benchmark tests novel pattern recognition — not business
decision synthesis. We chose based on task fit, not prestige."

2B — Why n8n for Orchestration

Short Version (15 seconds)

"For orchestration, we self-hosted n8n on AWS. n8n is open-source,
supports 400-plus integrations, and gives us a visual pipeline canvas
you can see right here — JD Agent, CV Agent, Scenario Agent, Decision Agent.
Each node is a real, independent API call with its own typed input and output.
This is not a single prompt. This is a modular decision system."

Full Version (for Q&A)

"We considered three orchestration approaches: custom JavaScript pipeline,
LangChain.js, and n8n. Here is why n8n won.

A custom pipeline gives us maximum control but produces nothing visually
demonstrable. LangChain.js is powerful but its agent abstraction makes
the pipeline invisible to an outside observer. Neither scores well on
the judging criterion that says 'make reasoning transparent.'

n8n is the only option that makes the multi-agent architecture
literally visible. When you see our n8n canvas, you are seeing
exactly what executes: four distinct nodes, each with a defined
input contract, a defined output contract, and explicit error handling.
The data flows from one agent to the next as structured JSON.
That is exactly what the hackathon brief called 'modular, explainable reasoning.'

For production credibility: n8n is used in enterprise environments
for multi-component AI pipelines connecting OpenAI, Anthropic,
and custom reasoning logic. Its LangChain-powered node system
natively supports memory management, tool-calling, and parallel
execution. At Perficient, a global consulting company, n8n is
deployed in production for AI agent workflows spanning Slack,
Jira, and ServiceNow — real enterprise-grade usage.

We self-host n8n on AWS ECS Fargate rather than using n8n Cloud
because free-tier execution limits would be a live risk during demo,
and because self-hosting on AWS allows us to demonstrate
production infrastructure skills.

We also evaluated Google Opal.
Google Opal is a no-code mini-app builder inside Google Labs.
It cannot perform multi-step API chaining, has no webhook handling,
no custom branching logic, and no production SLA guarantees.
Multiple independent analyses conclude it is a prototyping tool,
not a production orchestration platform.
The comparison is not close."

2C — Why AWS ECS Fargate (not Vercel, not EKS)

Short Version (15 seconds)

"We deployed our orchestration layer on AWS ECS Fargate with an
Application Load Balancer, infrastructure defined as code using AWS CDK.
United Airlines used this exact stack to launch a customer-facing
feature serving millions of users in under 60 days.
The PGA TOUR runs it to process four billion records per event.
For our use case — one stateful container at moderate scale —
ECS Fargate with CDK is the correct production-grade choice."

Full Version (for Q&A)

"Let me address the infrastructure decision directly.

We chose AWS ECS Fargate over three alternatives: Vercel, AWS EKS,
and Railway.

Vercel is excellent at what it does — frontend deployment —
but it abstracts all infrastructure from the developer.
A judge reviewing our GitHub would see a platform configuration,
not an infrastructure decision.
We wanted to demonstrate that we can reason about cloud architecture,
not just push to a platform.

AWS EKS — Kubernetes — is the correct choice for large-scale
microservices architectures requiring multi-cluster orchestration,
custom scheduling, and cross-cloud portability.
But EKS setup alone requires cluster creation, node group configuration,
IAM role setup, namespace configuration, persistent volume claims,
and ingress controller installation.
For a single stateful service during a 72-hour hackathon,
EKS introduces complexity that adds risk without adding value.

An engineering article published in March 2026 in sysops on Medium
puts it precisely: 'The difference between ECS and EKS is not capability —
both are mature, production-grade platforms. It is organisational
alignment and operational surface area.'
Our alignment is simple: one service, one region, predictable load.
ECS Fargate is the correct abstraction.

And ECS Fargate is genuinely production-grade, not a toy.
AWS's own customer page documents United Airlines using ECS Fargate
to serve millions of passengers during weather disruptions —
quote: 'we came up with the idea and took it all the way to
implementation in less than 60 days.'
Smartsheet uses ECS Fargate to reduce deployment time from hours to minutes.
The PGA TOUR runs thousands of simulations processing
nearly four billion records on ECS Fargate per event.

Our infrastructure is defined as code in AWS CDK TypeScript — committed to GitHub.
Any judge can read exactly what we deployed: the Fargate task definition,
the ALB listener rules, the Secrets Manager references for API keys.
That is the kind of transparency that earns the Technical Implementation score."

One developer, one context, one task at a time.
Cursor is genuinely excellent at intelligent code suggestion within that model.

Antigravity also natively supports Claude Sonnet 4.6 alongside Gemini 3.1 Pro,

PART 4 — ANTICIPATED JUDGE QUESTIONS & ANSWERS

These are the hardest questions you will face. Have these ready.

Q: "Why not use Gemini 3.1 Pro since it leads on most benchmarks?"

"Gemini 3.1 Pro leads on abstract reasoning benchmarks — ARC-AGI-2 at 77.1%,
GPQA Diamond — which measure novel pattern recognition and scientific depth.
Our agents perform structured business reasoning and evidence-based scoring,
which is the GDPval-AA category. On GDPval-AA, Sonnet 4.6 scores 1,633 Elo
versus Gemini's 1,317 — a gap of 316 points. We matched the model to the task.
Additionally, Gemini 3.1 Pro is still in preview as of March 27, 2026,
with documented instability under load. That is not a risk we take in a live demo."

Q: "Is this really multi-agent or just one prompt split into sections?"

"Each of our four agents is a separate API call with a distinct system prompt,
a typed input contract, and a typed JSON output contract.
Agent 1 produces a weight matrix. Agent 2 consumes that weight matrix and
produces per-candidate scores. Agent 3 consumes all scores and produces
a sourcing recommendation. Agent 4 consumes the raw profiles and produces
a cross-scenario delta table independently of Agent 3.
You can see each node execute sequentially in our n8n execution log.
Each node has its own latency, its own token count, its own error state.
This is a pipeline, not a prompt."

Q: "Why not use a more powerful model like Opus 4.6 for accuracy?"

"Opus 4.6 is priced at five times Sonnet 4.6 — $15 versus $3 per million
input tokens. On the tasks our agents perform, Sonnet 4.6 statistically
outperforms Opus 4.6 on GDPval-AA by 27 Elo points.
Using Opus would cost more and produce demonstrably worse outputs
on our specific workload category. That is not a cost-saving decision.
That is a model selection decision based on published benchmarks."

Q: "How do you handle the ethical concern of AI making hiring decisions?"

"We are explicit that ContextHire does not make hiring decisions.
It surfaces structured evidence to support a human decision.
The final call always stays with the hiring committee.
A peer-reviewed study in the Journal of Taylor & Francis (2025)
on AI in recruitment concludes that the highest-risk scenario is
AI operating without human oversight — and the most effective approach
is AI that makes its reasoning transparent so humans can interrogate it.
Every score in our system has a one-line evidence statement.
Every recommendation has a risk caveat.
We augment the decision. We do not replace it."

Q: "Your data is synthetic. How is this useful in the real world?"

"Synthetic data is the correct approach for a decision intelligence prototype
for two reasons: legal compliance and demonstration clarity.
Using real executive data without consent violates GDPR — the hackathon brief
explicitly prohibits this.
But the synthetic data we built is designed to exhibit realistic tensions:
candidates who are optimal for one scenario and suboptimal for another.
The value of the system is not in the specific candidates.
It is in the decision architecture — the ability to surface scenario-dependent
differentiation that gut-feel rating systems cannot produce.
In a production deployment, this system would connect to BMW's HRIS.
The agents, the weights, and the scenario logic stay identical."
